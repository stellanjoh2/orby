About Orby

Orby is a completely free, zero-install, drag-and-drop 3D model viewer that makes any model look stunning in seconds. It's perfect for AI-generated 3D (Meshy, Tripo, Luma, CSM, etc.), game assets, product visualization, and everything in between.

No accounts, no waiting, no uploads—just drop your file and get a full artist-grade studio: HDR environments, Look Filter Presets, a Luminance Tone Curve, Shader Lab stylized materials, texture map preview, SVG & font extrusion, real depth of field, selective bloom, film grain, tonemapping, 3-point lighting, exports from stills and vectors to turntable video, and pro controls that react instantly.

This project is an ongoing experiment in vibe-coding and self-directed learning. Coming from a design background with no formal development experience, I use Orby as a canvas to explore modern web technologies and real-time 3D on the web.

Feature FAQ
  • • Transform Widgets — Move, rotate, and scale your 3D model with visual gizmos. Click the mesh to show the rotate gizmo, or use shortcuts: W translate, E rotate, R scale (same as Blender), and Q to exit tools (select). Only one transform tool is active at a time.
  • • Display Modes — Shaded: Shows your model with realistic lighting and materials. Unlit: Displays your model's textures and colors without any lighting effects—everything appears evenly lit, like a flat illustration. Clay: Renders your model in a single color, great for focusing on shape and form. Wireframe: Shows only the edges and structure of your model. Press 1–4 to switch (Shaded, Unlit, Clay, Wireframe). You can also enable a wireframe overlay on any display mode, or hide the mesh entirely—press X to cycle overlay modes.
  • • Material Controls — Brightness: Makes your model lighter or darker. Metalness: Controls how metallic the surface looks (0 = plastic, 1 = chrome). Roughness: Adjusts surface smoothness (0 = mirror-like, 1 = matte). Emissive: Adds glowing light to materials, perfect for sci-fi effects, neon signs, or any glowing elements.
  • • Fresnel — Adds a colored glow around the edges of your model when viewed at an angle. Think of it like rim lighting that follows the silhouette. Great for making objects pop or creating a stylized look.
  • • HDRI (Environment) — A 360° image that lights your model and drives reflections. Built-in presets ship as JPEG environments — already graded and tuned — so the app stays fast to load and the download stays small. We still wanted you to own the scene, so you can upload your own .jpg, .png, .hdr, or .exr files: JPEGs behave like a finished photo sky; .hdr and .exr maps keep a wider brightness range, so sun and windows can punch harder on metal and glass (ease off Intensity and Exposure if it gets hot). Use a 2:1 equirectangular map. Custom uploads reset mood tints to neutral; re-upload the file after a refresh — it isn’t stored in scene JSON.
  • • 3-Point Lighting — Professional lighting setup with four lights: Key (main light), Fill (softens shadows), Rim (separates object from background), and Ambient (overall scene brightness). Adjust each light's color, intensity, and position. The key light supports Gobo projection—patterned shadows from palm, leaf, tree, and other presets. Drag with Alt + right-click to rotate the rig; Shift + right-click rotates the HDRI dome.
  • • Post-Processing Effects — Bloom: Adds a glowing, dreamy effect to bright areas. Depth of Field: Blurs the background while keeping your model sharp (like a camera lens). Film Grain: Adds texture for a cinematic feel. Chromatic Aberration: Splits color channels slightly at the edges. Lens Flare: Realistic light flares from bright sources. Light Rays: Volumetric god rays that follow the lens-flare sun direction. Lens Dirt: Simulates smudges on a camera lens for atmosphere. Ambient Occlusion (N8AO): Screen-space contact shadows—GPU-heavy; off by default. Fisheye Lens: Barrel distortion post effect. Color & Tone (contrast, temperature, H/S/L, highlights/shadows, clarity, fade, sharpness, vignette) and tone mapping (e.g. ACES) sit in the Camera & FX tab alongside exposure.
  • • Camera Controls — Orbit: Click and drag to rotate around your model. Pan: Right-click and drag to move the view. Zoom: Scroll or middle-click and drag. Focus: Press F to automatically frame your model. Auto Orbit: Automatically orbits the camera around your model—cycle Off / Slow / Fast with Space or use the Camera & FX tab (disabled while Isometric Camera is on). Handheld: Procedural camera shake (Off / Subtle / Strong). Isometric Camera: RTS-style locked view with presets; pan optional via Unlock. Exposure & Auto Exposure: Manual exposure sets overall brightness; optional auto exposure adapts to the scene. Use the Histogram and Curve fold-outs, Look Filters, and the rest of Color & Tone for a full grade.
  • • Histogram — A graph showing the brightness distribution in your image. Helps you see if your render is too dark, too bright, or overexposed. The histogram turns orange or red when parts of your image are too bright (overexposed), warning you to lower the exposure. Toggle it on from the Camera & FX tab; it sits in a fold-out under the heading, same idea as the curve (see below).
  • • Tone Curve — A luminance response curve with four draggable handles—output at black, two mid-tones, and output at white (Catmull–Rom-style tangents, baked lookup table in the grade). It shapes how tones map before the rest of color grading—use it for contrast roll-off, lifted blacks, or punchy mids. Open the Curve fold-out under Camera & FX (next to the histogram); your curve is saved in Copy scene / .orby like other settings.
  • • Look Filter Presets — Under Camera & FX → Look Filters, pick a thumbnail preset for a one-tap look: None, Studio, Noir, Mood, Vintage, Cinema, Frost, Golden, Dream, and Neon combine color grade, tone curve, bloom, grain, chromatic aberration, and more. None resets those camera/FX defaults while keeping framing (FOV, tilt, orbit). If you tweak sliders afterward, the selection shows as custom. Preset state is included when you copy or paste scene JSON.
  • • Base & Grid — Add a solid base platform or grid floor to your scene. Great for product presentations or architectural renders. The base can be scaled and colored, and the grid helps with alignment and perspective.
  • • Scene Settings — Copy and paste all your settings (lighting, camera, effects, materials, tone curve, Look Filter selection, histogram/curve panel toggles, SVG & font extrude, and more) as JSON. Perfect for saving your favorite setups or sharing them with others. Load a scene to instantly apply all settings at once.
SVG Extrude

Orby can convert SVG fills into 3D meshes. After importing an SVG, use the Object → SVG Extrude block to control form and shading.

  • Depth: Sets global extrusion depth for all SVG fills.
  • Angle: Controls normal smoothing (lower = faceted edges, higher = smoother highlights).
  • Flip Direction: Inverts front/back extrusion direction.
  • Color Override: Replaces imported SVG fill colors with a single chosen color.
  • Per-Color Depth/Position: Fine-tune each detected fill color independently for layered logo effects.
  • Surface presets: Optional procedural surface detail (scale and strength) on extruded meshes.
  • Scene Settings: SVG extrude controls are included when copying or pasting scene settings.
  • SVG to GLB Export: Exported SVG meshes are saved as opaque GLB materials for predictable shading across viewers.
Generate from Font

Type live text and extrude it into a 3D mesh—no SVG required. Open Object → Generate from Font (below mesh stats on the Object tab).

  • Live preview: Edit text, pick a font family and style, adjust tracking, kerning, line height, and alignment before generating.
  • Custom fonts: Load .ttf / .otf files, or use built-in families.
  • Extrusion: Depth, bevel, smoothing angle, fill color, and mesh detail—same post-generate controls as SVG extrude.
  • Reveal animations: Play slide-in or other reveal types on the extruded text with scrub, loop, and duration controls.
  • Scene Settings: Font extrude state is included when copying or pasting scene settings.
Maps Preview

After loading a textured model, the Object → Maps grid lists detected texture slots (albedo, normal, roughness, metallic, emissive, and packed ORM/MR maps).

  • Click a thumbnail to pin a live map preview on the mesh in the viewport.
  • Floating panel: Opens a larger preview with channel tabs; drag to reposition. Press Esc to close.
  • FBX Map Slots: For FBX imports, pick a material target and assign maps per part when textures were not embedded.
  • Display modes: Re-click the active display mode icon to unpins the preview; switching modes clears the overlay.
Transparency & Glass Issues

Why do transparent surfaces look wrong, flicker, or show “see-through” layers?

This is a known challenge in real-time 3D rendering for glTF / GLB files on the web. Most web-based 3D viewers are built on WebGL and Three.js, so these limitations apply broadly.

Why does this happen?

Real-time engines sort draw calls (meshes or batches) — not individual triangles. When transparent surfaces share a single mesh and material, they can’t be perfectly ordered, causing artifacts like flickering, “see-through” layers, or surfaces fighting each other. This is especially common with marketplace downloads, where unrelated transparent surfaces are often packed into one blended atlas.

Why does the same file look different in other viewers?

Dedicated platforms use tuned, proprietary renderers optimized for their own content. Other viewers won’t match them pixel-for-pixel unless they make the same rendering tradeoffs.

What about refractive glass?

Thin glass panes in glTF typically use the KHR_materials_transmission extension. When supported, this glass reads directly from the file’s own material properties rather than being overridden by global scene settings.

Are there settings I can adjust?

Under Object → Advanced, Transparency Mode and Stabilize Layered Transparency change how blended surfaces are drawn — but they can’t fix assets where unrelated surfaces are packed into a single blended material. Expand Rare Fixes — Transmission & Glass for heuristic glass controls (opacity, tint, flip normal Y, front faces only). For best results, authors should split meshes, use MASK over BLEND where possible, and use KHR_materials_transmission for proper glass.

Performance

Orby is a real-time GPU renderer built on WebGL and Three.js. Every frame, the scene is drawn and then post-processing effects are applied as full-screen “passes”. The more heavy effects you stack, and the higher your resolution, the more work your GPU has to do each frame.

  • Very cheap (almost free): exposure, contrast, saturation, tint, temperature, fade, and the luminance tone curve (evaluated in the same color pass). These are light math per pixel.
  • Medium cost: vignette, clarity, sharpness, Fresnel, fisheye lens. These add extra calculations or an extra pass, but are usually fine on modern GPUs.
  • Heavier effects: depth of field, bloom, lens flare, Light Rays, lens dirt, film grain, chromatic aberration, FXAA, Ambient Occlusion (N8AO), and some histogram/auto-exposure work. These often require additional buffers and multiple full-screen passes and will have the biggest impact on frame rate.

Turning an effect on is usually the biggest step in cost. Increasing intensity (for example, bloom strength from 0.3 to 0.6) changes the look more than the performance. The real performance lever is how many heavy effects you run at the same time.

If Orby feels slow, start by disabling the heavier effects (Depth of Field, Bloom, Light Rays, N8AO, Lens Dirt, Grain, Chromatic Aberration, FXAA), or run the browser window a bit smaller so the internal render resolution is lower. For quick look-dev, stick to the cheaper color & tone controls first, then enable the cinematic effects at the end when you are close to final.
